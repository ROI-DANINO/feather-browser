# My Own Gym

Yesterday I deleted nine days of work — taught the agent to *look* human enough to slip past bot-detectors, then proved to myself it was a treadmill and cut it. I wrote about it. I felt good.

Then I slept on it, read some AI news, and woke up wanting to build the whole thing back.

This is the story of catching that — and finding the thing I actually wanted underneath it.

## The morning-after swing

I came in hot. *Remake Feather around stealth. Switch the engine to Firefox — I'd heard Firefox is sneakier. Go all-in on teaching agents to beat bot-detection. This is the cutting edge, don't hold me back.*

Twelve hours earlier I'd cut exactly that, with evidence, and written a note to future-me telling him not to restart it. Future-me showed up the next morning and tried to restart it.

So before I let myself run, I asked the AI to do the one thing I keep training it to do: don't flatter me. And it put the mirror right in my face — *you decided the opposite of this last night, with your own data. Is this a new reason, or just excitement?* That stung, because it was fair.

I didn't want to win the argument by vibes, so I sent a research team out to settle the facts. It came back blunt:

- **"Firefox is stealthier" is a myth.** The stealth people credit to Firefox actually comes from a pile of patches bolted *on top* of it — and the big detectors already recognize that exact disguise by name. The engine doesn't save you.
- **I can't win this fight.** The companies that catch bots look at *trillions* of signals a day. A solo guy patching a browser doesn't out-run that. My own data had already said as much.

So the swing was just a swing. Good to know. But that left the real question: if not stealth, then what — and why was I so drawn to it in the first place?

## The thing I actually wanted

I went back and looked at what had genuinely been *fun*. Not "runs my errands for me" — that bored me. The most alive I'd felt on this whole project was a few days ago, watching my agents go up against real bot-detection test pages and *pass*. Seeing it work. That was the hook.

And then I drew a line I'm proud of, on my own: **I don't want to sneak my agents into websites that don't want them.** That's the unwinnable fight *and* the wrong side of it. What I want is to watch my agents get good — pass real, hard tests — in a place where that's *allowed.*

Which is just… a gym. Build my own arena. Stand up the real detection systems myself, point my own agents at them, and watch them learn to pass. A מכון ווינגייט — a training academy — for browser agents. Nobody's club I'm breaking into. My own ground, my own rules, my own bots.

The thing I'd been reaching for with "stealth" was never the disguise. It was the *scoreboard.* I just had it pointed at other people's doors instead of my own gym.

## What "pioneer" actually meant to me

I'd been calling myself a pioneer, and the AI gently pointed out other people have built things like this before. But that's not what I meant. I don't need to be *first.* I want to be *here* — doing this *now*, while agentic AI is climbing toward its peak and not there yet. In the room while it happens.

And I had to be honest with myself about the goal, which quietly changes everything: **I'm not doing this for money.** I'm doing it to *learn* — by building it myself, which is the only way things stick for me — to have something real to show, maybe to get a job in this field, and because working at the edge of this stuff is the most fun I've ever had with a computer. Once I said that out loud, the "you can't win commercially" worry just… stopped mattering. I'm not trying to win a market. I'm trying to get great and have proof.

## Everyone talks about harnesses. I build my own.

Here's the part that actually moved me.

I've got more than one project going, and lately the spread has been *tiring*. A philosophy I wrote. A framework on top of it. An orchestration "brain." And this browser. Four things, four contexts, and a low hum of *this is slipping out of my hands.*

On my break it hit me: maybe these aren't four things. Maybe they're one machine — a harness — and I just never said it out loud.

I asked the AI to read across all of them and tell me honestly. And it found something better than a hunch: I'd *already* been building toward exactly that, on paper, for weeks — I just hadn't let myself see the whole line. The philosophy is the soul. The framework is the cockpit. The brain is the orchestrator. And the browser is the *body* — the hands and eyes the rest of it drives. My tiredness wasn't "too many scattered projects." It was laying track toward one destination without looking up to see the rails connect.

But it gave me the honest catch too, and I needed it: **the browser is a body the machine drives — not a limb to fuse into it.** The temptation was to weld everything into one giant thing. That's the exact trap I'm scared of, the everything-machine that slips through your fingers. Even my own framework's rules say *absorb only the slice you actually use.* So: the brain *drives* the browser. It doesn't swallow it.

And the gym? The gym is the **wire.** It's the first real job that needs both halves at once — a body to run the bots in, and a brain to score them. Build the gym, and the two finally touch *because the work demands it* — no big risky merge, just a thin connection that earns its place. The thing I couldn't picture — "how do I connect these?" — turns out you don't. You build something that needs both, and the connection draws itself.

## The honest catch (there's always one)

A gym you grade yourself can lie to you. If I only ever test my agents against detectors *I* control and can quietly tune to pass, I've built a trophy factory, not a training ground — the exact "green checkmarks instead of truth" trap I swore off months ago. So the rule is baked in from the start: test against detectors whose insides I *don't* control, and design tests that are *allowed to fail.* A pass only counts if I didn't get to grade it.

No code today. I wrote down the whole direction so a future me can't lose it, and stopped at the line where the *fun* part starts — designing the smallest version of the gym that's already alive enough to watch.

I came in wanting to build a better disguise to sneak into someone else's club. I'm leaving with the blueprint for my own gym — and the quiet realization that everything I've been building was always one machine. I just had to stop running long enough to see it.

🔗 **LinkedIn cut**

I woke up wanting to undo the best decision I'd made all week.

The night before, I'd cut nine days of "stealth" work on my AI browser — teaching an agent to look human enough to dodge bot-detectors — because my own data proved it was a treadmill I couldn't win. Then I slept, read some AI news, and came in hot to build it all back, this time "the right way."

So I did what I now do with any decision I can't trust myself on: I asked the AI team I use to keep my code honest to keep *me* honest. It held up the mirror — *you're reversing your own evidence on a wave of excitement.* It was right.

But under the swing was something real. The most fun I've had on this project was watching my agents go up against hard tests and *pass.* I didn't want to sneak them past sites that don't want them — that's a fight I can't win and the wrong side of it anyway. I wanted to watch them get good somewhere that's allowed.

Which is just a gym. So I'm building one. My own arena, my own detectors, my own bots — a training academy for browser agents, where a pass only counts if I didn't get to grade it.

And building it, I finally saw that my four scattered projects — a philosophy, a framework, an orchestration brain, and the browser — were secretly one machine the whole time. The tiredness wasn't too many projects. It was not looking up long enough to see the rails connect.

I'm not doing this for money. I'm doing it to get great, in the open, while this field is still being built.

Sometimes the best work of the day is aiming.
